# 01 — Core Concept & Vision (v0.2)

## 1. High Concept
**Aetheria** is a world woven from living magic, and for a thousand years it has been pulling souls in from other worlds. You are the newest one. The Goddess of Fate grants you a **Status**. The Adventurers' Guild stamps a wooden card with an **F**, and after that nobody is telling you what to do.

The game takes the whole isekai/high-fantasy genre and builds it as **one hand-built 3D world** where every trope is a playable system. You can play the summoned hero, the reincarnated slime, the exiled noble with a "useless" skill, the frontier farmer, the dungeon diver, the dragon tamer, or the next Demon Overlord.

## 2. Pillars
1. **Every adventure fantasy is playable.** Each fantasy needs its own systems, rewards and story beats, not just a cosmetic label.
2. **Rank is the language of the world.** F–SSS appears on your card, on monsters, quests, zones, materials, spells and gear.
3. **Wonder over efficiency.** The world is not a checklist. Every region has secrets that only curiosity finds.
4. **From nobody to legend.** The power curve should feel *absurd* by the end. Early wolves were deadly; late-game you scare dragons.
5. **Systems that collide.** Class + Sub-class + Unique Skill + gear + companions + titles stack into builds that "break" the game on purpose.
6. **A world that remembers.** Settlements, companions, factions and ecology respond to what you do. Named monsters are hand-authored and change with region state and story flags; they do not gain personal scars, titles or vendettas from defeating you (Canon §4d).

## 3. The "Fantasy Feel" Rules (applies to every department)
| Rule | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Magic is visible and alive** | Ley lines glow faintly at night, mana motes drift in forests, spells leave scorch marks and frost that lingers |
| **Every place has a postcard moment** | A planned vista: first sight of the World Tree, the floating islands at sunrise, the red moon over the Demon Realm |
| **Everything has a story** | Every item, monster and skill gets flavor text. No "Iron Sword (+5)" without a line of lore |
| **NPCs live in the world** | Schedules, gossip, reactions to your rank, titles, companions and reputation |
| **Music tells you where you are** | Each region has a leitmotif; each companion has a theme; battle music escalates by enemy rank |
| **Cozy and epic coexist** | A dragon fight at noon, a hot spring and a stew with your party at night |
| **Wonder is earned** | The coolest things are optional and hidden: a sleeping titan, a library at the bottom of a lake, a cat that is secretly a god |

## 4. Gameplay Loops
- **Moment-to-moment (seconds):** stylish third-person action combat. Dodge, parry, chain skills, swap targets, call companion arts.
- **Activity (5–20 min):** guild contract → travel (discover things on the way) → resolve (fight, talk, sneak, tame) → loot → return.
- **Session (1–3 hrs):** clear a dungeon, finish a quest chain, class-change, upgrade the home, fall into a rabbit hole of secrets.
- **Campaign:** main story ~200–250 hrs at a focused pace (~600–800 hrs exploring everything); F → SSS and the secret ending ≈ 1,000+ hrs. Six acts, companion arcs, the Great Labyrinth, 11 endings + 1 secret.

### Endings
**11 main endings + 1 secret (The Thirteenth Star, ~1,000 hrs)**, with epilogue variations bringing the total to ~100. The full list and requirements live in `02_Lore/story.md` → Endings. Headline examples: *The Hero's Dawn* (side with the gods), *The Crimson Throne* (become the Demon Overlord), *The Unwoven Sky* (destroy the System), *The Quiet Field* (walk away), *The Harvest* (the beautiful bad ending).

**Afterstory:** after a world-saving ending, players can keep living in Aetheria in a peaceful open-ended state. Explore, hunt, spend time with companions, pursue crafting/farming/shop paths and help regions recover. New adventures remain optional, with the main world crisis resolved.

## 7. Scope Philosophy for a Two-Person Team
**Design wide, build in rings.** This bible describes the *whole* dream. We build outward in rings (see Production): first a polished frontier region, then each new region adds a rank band. Data-driven content means a new monster, quest or item is a new row of data, not new code.

## 8. The Player Experience Contract

This section translates the creative brief into checks that can guide writing, combat, world design and production. These are direction and targets; Canon remains authoritative when a detail is locked.

### Emotional sequence

The intended emotional center is **curious → vulnerable → hopeful → capable → connected**. A new area should invite the player to ask a question; danger should make the answer feel earned; allies and visible recovery should let the player feel that their effort mattered. Awe, fear, grief and triumph can all appear, but the ordinary world should retain warmth and possibility.

The player is not special because a prophecy excuses every choice. They become important through accumulated acts: accepting a contract, learning a creature, keeping a promise, choosing who to protect and returning to places they have changed. Old-world experience is a lens for dialogue and practical problem-solving, while Aetheria becomes the character’s lived home.

### The hour-sized adventure

An ordinary play session should naturally combine the activities the player asked to emphasize: exploration and combat form the core; investigation, conversation, preparation, crafting and travel give them texture. The game should not require the player to finish a long chain to feel that an hour mattered.

**Good stopping points** include a discovered landmark, a returned contract, a new clue in the journal, a crafted improvement, a companion conversation, a rescued settlement or a visible change in a familiar route. Save-safe pauses belong at hubs, camps, quest resolutions and other natural transitions. These are design goals, not a promise that every hour grants a level or major story beat.

### Combat quality bar

Combat must read clearly at the normal camera distance and feel responsive at close range. The player should feel a reliable connection between input, anticipation, contact and recovery: attacks have weight, movement keeps momentum, hit reactions communicate force, and camera effects support the action without obscuring it.

- **Ordinary threats** teach one clear behavior and let the player demonstrate a learned answer quickly.
- **Elites** combine a small number of known behaviors in new timings, spacing or terrain.
- **Bosses** test observation, pattern learning, preparation and decision-making. A player who does not parry should still have a fair route through dodging, guarding, spacing, interrupts, companions and build choices.
- **Flash belongs to consequence.** Large effects should mark a meaningful skill, reaction, phase change or story beat; routine attacks need clean silhouettes and readable tells.
- **Build identity matters.** Weapon, class, element and skill choices should change the player’s rhythm, reach, risk and recovery rather than only inflate damage.

The first playable slice must prove a complete short loop: move through a readable space, discover a threat, learn its tell, fight with a satisfying combo, recover, and see a reward. High-rank spectacle depends on this foundation.

### Exploration and travel

Every trip should carry at least one reason to look away from the objective: a landmark, an environmental clue, a creature behavior, a shortcut, a conversation, a resource or a view worth remembering. Not every trip needs a battle, and not every side path needs a collectible. Repeated travel should become more comfortable through knowledge, relationships, roads, abilities and carefully limited teleportation.

Danger should change the *quality* of attention. Safe routes invite scenic observation and small discoveries; hostile frontiers make sound, sightlines, shelter, weather and escape routes matter. The player should be able to read the world without a checklist covering every hidden detail.

Vertical routes should reward curiosity and character growth. Climbing, gliding and later movement abilities can reveal new approaches, but exploration must remain navigable for players who prefer the obvious path. A hidden route should be a discovery, not a mandatory execution test.

### Investigation and preparation

Monster knowledge is an adventure tool. A hunt can begin with incomplete testimony, tracks, damage to a place, rumors or a witnessed behavior. Evidence should narrow possibilities and change the player’s plan: where to search, what element to avoid, what time to travel, or whether a creature is defending a territory. The journal should distinguish **observed fact**, **local belief** and **unconfirmed theory**.

Preparation should feel like a brief, satisfying choice, not a maintenance tax. Food offers modest boosts; equipment, antidotes, light sources and companion roles matter when they answer a known risk. The player should never have to eat every few minutes or rebuild a loadout for routine travel.

### Towns, people and consequence

A town should provide safety that the player can feel: readable guards, open services, useful rumor, faces that remember them and a reason to return. Consequences should become visible at different speeds. A failed public defense can change the day’s mood immediately; rebuilding, trade and trust can evolve over subsequent in-game days and weeks.

The world’s reaction must be proportionate. A missed ordinary contract can cost trust or money; a major promise can alter alliances and lives. The game should explain the stakes before irreversible choices, and long-term consequences should have intermediate feedback so players can understand cause and effect.

### Companions as people

Companions are AI-controlled allies, not extra player characters. They should contribute through readable roles, useful reactions and opinions that remain their own. A companion can challenge the player without blocking play; disagreement creates conversation and consequence rather than a hidden test the player must guess.

The party’s daily life is part of the fantasy: banter during travel, focused comments during a hunt, a meal after danger and quiet acknowledgment after loss. Keep these moments short enough to preserve the player’s pace, and reserve longer scenes for earned relationships or pivotal choices. Permanent combat death remains opt-in under Canon §5; relationship writing should not imply otherwise.

### Aetheria’s daily life

The in-game calendar should create texture—markets at dawn, work and training in daylight, social life in the evening, and greater exposure to danger after dark—without asking players to wait in real time. Each schedule should improve a useful play decision: finding a person, seeing a service, witnessing a local event or understanding who depends on whom.

The economy should be legible at the point of choice. Show prices before purchase, distinguish essentials from luxuries, and communicate what a companion or service payment covers. Scarcity can create roleplay, but routine upkeep must not quietly threaten a long campaign or punish players for exploring.

### World-state feedback

Liberation should alter more than a banner. The player should notice safer roads, changed board contracts, repaired spaces, relieved residents, new visitors and a festival or other public acknowledgment. The old threat can leave a story scar without continuing its attack cycle. Ordinary ecology, calamities and authored aftermaths remain available as separate event types.

Use a clear chain for every lasting change: **action → recorded world state → visible response → later opportunity**. If a choice has a major cost, communicate it through people and events rather than a vague morality score. If a system cannot afford bespoke dialogue for every permutation, reuse authored response families with a few high-value character-specific callbacks.

### Anime presentation

The anime feeling comes from pacing and emotional punctuation as much as from effects: a sharp entrance, a companion’s look before a difficult fight, a quiet breath after a victory, a memorable reveal and a return to ordinary life. Camera flourishes and the per-Act opening should celebrate the player’s actual journey, but remain skippable and should never require an unearned spoiler.

Music uses instrumental regional identity as the foundation and stronger rock or vocal energy for major battles. A shared musical motif can change harmony and instrumentation as the player’s relationship with a place changes. The interface should feel like a clear in-world Status system while keeping combat information readable and customizable.

### Scope and quality gates

For a two-person team, breadth is a content pipeline, not a promise to hand-author every possible branch. Build one complete example of a system, test whether it creates the intended feeling, then scale through reusable data, encounter patterns and short authored callbacks.

Before a feature spreads beyond its first region, answer:

1. What player feeling and decision does it serve?
2. What data and reusable rules make it work?
3. What does the player see when it succeeds, fails or is ignored?
4. How does it respect accessibility and non-parry play?
5. What is the smallest testable version, and what can be cut without damaging the core fantasy?

An idea belongs in the full dream only when it strengthens the world. It belongs in the next build only when the smallest version can be made, evaluated and improved.
