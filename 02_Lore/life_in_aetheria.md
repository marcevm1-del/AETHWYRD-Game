# 02d — Life in Aetheria: Everything In Between (v0.3)

## 1. Otherworlders in Society
- Everyone knows otherworlders exist. They're called **"Veilborn"**, or "Fallers" as slang.
- **Public opinion:** heroes in legend, oddities in person. Veilborn are often clumsy with customs, say strange words ("OK," "mayonnaise," "level up"), and grow frighteningly fast.
- **The Veilborn Registry:** the Church quietly tracks every known otherworlder. Not all of them were summoned on purpose. Rifts pull people through accidentally (the Transported).
- **Famous Veilborn inventions** (Kaede and others): the guild rank letters, playing cards, the "bath culture" in Aldmere, mayonnaise (in every tavern, endlessly debated), the Guild's quest-board format.
- Other living Veilborn you can meet: a retired Veilborn chef in Port Goldmere, a Veilborn conspiracy theorist in Aldmere's slums, and Kaito's classmates.

## 2. The Adventurers' Guild
- **Founded** 12 SC by Kaede Aoyama under the **Wanderer's Charter**: *"No birth, race, or crown shall stand between a soul and its rising."*
- **Neutral by law** in wars between nations, though in practice it leans on Aldmere.
- **Structure:** Grand Guildmaster (Aldmere) → regional Guildmasters → branch masters → examiners → receptionists. Mina is beloved in every branch.
- **Rules:** no killing other adventurers (dueling at the arena only); report new monster species; obey Emergency Calls; quests up to one rank above yours.
- **Traditions:**
  - Your first copper is pinned to the guild wall.
  - Rookies buy the hall a round at E-Rank.
  - A dead adventurer's card is hung in the **Hall of Cards**.
- **How guild members talk:** see [[guild_hall_talk_and_lexicon]] for practical board, hunt, pay, core-handling and party vocabulary; sample conversations; ambient-dialogue rules. Guild jargon is learned in context, not delivered as a glossary speech.
- **Guild slang:** "card-burner" (someone who died at F), "goblin-chewed" (badly beaten), "silver-sick" (C-ranks who never rise), "a Kaede" (someone who levels ridiculously fast).

## 3. Religion & Daily Faith
- The **Holy Church of Lumina** officially honors all Eight, but its doctrine centers on **Solenne** (justice) and **Ilvaris** (fate).
- **Common practices:** morning sun-blessings, Lantern Night offerings to the dead, coin tosses to Vex before travel, and Thorgrim's hammer-marks on new tools.
- **Heresies:** Predation (devouring skills), Forbidden magic, worship of the Forgotten Ninth, and "System questioning."
- **The Inquisition:** Church agents with anti-magic training. They hunt heretics and, secretly, inconvenient Veilborn.
- **Temples per god:** Solenne's are sun-domes, Maelis's are moon-pools, Thorgrim's are forge-halls, Sylvara's are living groves, Kaelthas's are storm-towers, Morrowyn's are lantern-crypts, Vex's are crossroad shrines with dice, and Ilvaris's are spindle-spires.

## 4. Law, Crime & Class
- **Aldmere:** feudal monarchy. Nobles (dukes → barons), knights, freemen, serfs. Adventurers sit oddly outside it; an S-Rank outranks most nobles socially.
- **Slavery:** outlawed in Aldmere since Hero #2, but still practiced in border baronies and by the Syndicate (especially beastkin). A major story thread.
- **Crime:** the **Shadow Syndicate** runs the underworld; bounties for criminals are posted on the guild board.
- **Player crime:** theft, trespass and assault give you bounties, Karma loss and guards; jail can be escaped, paid off or served (time skip).

## 5. Economy & Daily Life
- **Coins:** copper → silver → gold → platinum → mithril coin, each denomination worth 100 of the previous one. A copper buys a small bite; silver covers ordinary lodging; gold buys good gear; platinum is for major property and contracts; mithril is reserved for exceptional fortunes and state-scale transactions. The wallet makes change automatically; see `Data/World/world_tuning.json` for exact values.
- **Monster cores** fuel everything: city lamps, forges, heating, magic tools. That's why monster hunting is an industry, and Rhaka's grievance.
- **Daily rhythm (90-min day):** dawn markets, noon guild rush, afternoon training, evening taverns, night watches and monster hours.
- **Food:** fresh bread, stews, grilled monster meat (horned rabbit is the classic), elven waybread, dwarven stout, Isles ramen and dumplings, desert spice skewers, Northfolk smoked fish. And mayonnaise.
- **Entertainment:** taverns, bards, card games (Veilborn import), the arena, festivals, the Academy's magic duels, the Brindlewick talent show.

## 7. Magic in Everyday Life
- Street lamps burn core-light; water pumps run on small rune engines.
- Healers in every town (Church-licensed). Unlicensed healers are illegal, like Elira.
- **Familiars** are common among the rich: tiny dragons, owls, slimes in jars.
- Children's **Status Appraisal Day** at age 12 is a coming-of-age festival, joyful or crushing depending on what's revealed.

## 8. Knowledge, Books & Lore Objects
- **200+ readable books** planned: history, myths, recipes, monster guides, romance novels (a popular in-world series, *The Knight and the Wyvern Princess*), Veilborn memoirs, Valdrisian fragments.
- **Lore tablets, murals, ghost memories, overheard gossip, tavern songs**: the world tells its story in layers, so players who dig find the truth before the plot reveals it.

### History belongs to people, not only archives

The deep chronology is collected in [[history]]. In daily life, history survives as a route someone refuses to travel, a family custom whose origin is half-forgotten, a Guild card no clerk will remove, a shrine maintained by people who disagree about the god, or a song whose verses change with the audience. The Church, Crown, Guild, Academy, clans, merchants and households each preserve different kinds of evidence; none should function as a universally neutral narrator.

When a local history matters to a quest, let the player meet at least two perspectives and one tangible trace. A royal record can establish what was ordered; a family account can establish what happened to the people affected; an altered landscape can show what neither witness understands. Conflicts in memory should lead to investigation or meaningful disagreement, not make every culture deceptive. People can be sincere, mistaken, selective, courageous or self-protective in any society.

The current chronology distinguishes three statuses: facts settled by Canon, events already present in the design bible but not fully locked, and connective proposals that need review. Collectibles and NPCs must respect those distinctions, especially where Act III–V secrets are concerned. The history is a designer-facing resource; public dialogue only reveals what a speaker could know.

## 9. What a Day Feels Like

The calendar is a rhythm tool, not a clock the player has to obey. With a 90-minute real-time day and a seven-day week, the world can move through recognizable phases while the player still chooses where to go. Dawn favors markets and departures; daylight favors work, travel and training; dusk brings the return to town; night changes patrols, gatherings and some monster behavior. Exact opening hours belong in schedules and data, not in prose that can drift out of date.

### The daily social loop

1. **Notice:** hear a rumor, see a board update, meet a returning traveler or observe a settlement’s current condition.
2. **Choose:** decide whether to take a contract, follow a lead, spend time with a person, rest, craft or keep traveling.
3. **Act:** the world clock advances through the chosen activity; characters who matter to the current scene respond consistently.
4. **Return:** the player sees what changed—someone went home, a shop closed, the inn filled, guards changed shifts or a rumor reached another settlement.

The loop should create opportunities, not punish a player for missing a single time window. Important services have a clear alternate route: a notice board, a message, another worker or a later visit. Time-sensitive scenes announce themselves with enough context to let the player decide.

### A simulation that can scale

The ambition is roughly 2,500 named NPCs, each with identity and a backstory. That does not mean simulating 2,500 full lives at maximum detail every frame. A scalable design separates what is persistent from what is active:

- **Identity layer:** every named resident has a stable name, role, home, affiliation and connection to a place or person.
- **Routine layer:** ordinary schedules can be represented as a small number of daily anchors—home, work, social place—with variations for local conditions.
- **Nearby layer:** detailed movement, conversation availability, reactions and witness checks run for people around the player or involved in an active scene.
- **Story layer:** a smaller cast gets authored state changes, personal arcs and carefully tracked callbacks.

When an NPC is off-screen, advance the relevant routine or settlement state in coarse steps. Do not pretend the character performed a precise sequence the game never simulated. Keep quest facts in quest/world state, not duplicated as a separate memory flag on every person. This preserves named identity while containing implementation and writing costs.

## 10. Towns as Places to Spend Time

A town earns its place in the player’s route when it offers safety, useful services, social texture and visible response. The player should be able to understand the layout after one visit: where to take a contract, where to sleep, where to buy or repair gear, where to hear local news and where people gather.

### Settlement routine

- **Morning:** deliveries, work, market setup and guild departures.
- **Day:** workshops, training, civic duties and public services.
- **Evening:** meals, performances, rumors, companion conversations and social scenes.
- **Night:** quieter streets, watches, late venues and region-dependent danger.

This is a broad staging rhythm, not a requirement that every NPC have four unique schedule points. One animation set, a small set of routine anchors and a few authored events can make a town feel occupied. Story-critical meetings override routine in an explainable way, then return characters to their normal pattern.

### Settlement state and visible change

Safety, prosperity, trust and scarcity should be understandable from what the player sees. A restored street has repaired props and open stalls; a safe road has ordinary travelers and fewer defensive barricades; a community under strain has shorter conversations, closed workspaces or a visible queue. The same state can adjust services, patrols, rumor content and encounter weights without needing a unique story branch for every resident.

Give a major public choice several scales of feedback:

1. **Immediate:** a witness, party member or official reacts.
2. **Local:** the relevant household, guild branch or settlement changes its routine or access.
3. **Long-term:** an alliance, regional opportunity or later story beat reflects the outcome.

Every large consequence should have a comprehensible cause and a chance to respond. Recovery does not erase harm; it gives the player a way to repair trust, provide restitution or accept that a relationship has changed.

## 11. Companion Money and the Cost of a Life

The player wants an economy they can touch: a room costs money, a meal costs money, and companions have needs rather than existing as free equipment. This can add intimacy and roleplay, but only if the payment system stays predictable and does not become a repetitive tax.

### Proposed upkeep structure

- Tell the player what a payment covers before they confirm it: meals, travel provisions, shared lodging or a personal stipend.
- Let the player settle support in convenient blocks at an inn, camp or other trusted hub. Avoid asking for money after every battle or every meal.
- Make a well-funded contribution cover a long, clearly named span; make a small contribution cover a shorter, clearly named need. Use the live economy data for values rather than hard-coding prose.
- Give companions personalities about money. One may insist on paying their share; another may save for a goal; another may value a shared meal more than a larger allowance.
- If the player cannot pay, start with a conversation and a practical alternative. Only a sustained, clearly communicated failure to provide agreed support can lead to a companion pausing travel or leaving.
- A departure is a relationship consequence, not a stealth deletion of a character or their questline. The player receives notice, can talk, and can later repair the relationship when the companion’s conditions allow.

**Anti-chore rule:** upkeep is checked at a visible weekly or journey milestone, not continuously. No companion should starve, die or lose accumulated bond progress because the player missed an opaque payment. The default experience must be recoverable; any harsher economic mode is an explicit choice.

### Inns, meals and ordinary spending

Inns are where money becomes visible comfort: a room provides rest, a meal gives a modest boost, a bath or social event can create a small scene, and the rumor board helps the player choose what to do next. The player should see the price and benefit together. The lowest-cost room remains safe and useful; expensive rooms add comfort or flavor rather than mandatory combat power.

Keep essentials, useful upgrades and luxuries visually distinct. The player can always compare a purchase with the value of a current contract. Money sources should come from play the player already enjoys—contracts, selling extra materials, tournaments and optional services—not from an unrelated grind loop.

## 12. Trust, Law and Rumor

Public reputation is local before it is global. A witness tells a nearby person; a guild report or merchant route carries a confirmed fact farther; rumor can arrive late or be wrong. The interface should separate **witnessed**, **reported** and **unverified** information so the player understands why a character reacts.

The law should have readable boundaries. Trespass, theft, assault and contract fraud need distinct reactions and recovery routes. A guard who saw an act can respond immediately; a distant settlement should not magically know about it without a messenger, a public notice or an authored story connection. A bounty is an invitation to resolve a consequence, not a hidden permanent penalty that follows the player everywhere.

For authored choices, use a compact set of reusable state families—trust, safety, scarcity, faction pressure, public witness, personal promise, grief/celebration and restoration. Each state needs a clear owner, when it changes, who can know it, what the player sees and how it can resolve or recover. This is a proposed implementation vocabulary, not a new canon rule.

## 13. Production Guardrails for Daily Life

- Put ordinary people in the world for a reason: livelihood, kinship, faith, work, trade or a relationship to the local conflict.
- Give each culture internal differences; avoid making a whole people speak, trade or worship as one person.
- Show a custom in action before explaining it in a book.
- Make prices and social stakes visible at the point of choice.
- Use authored scenes for the few relationships that carry the emotional story; use reusable routines and role-based responses for the wider population.
- Let a peaceful evening be a meaningful reward after danger, not a screen the player must click through.
- Keep consequential story facts distinct from everyday rumor, and protect Canon §3 spoilers from casual ambient dialogue.

**Playtest question:** after one in-game week, can a new player name one place that feels safe, one person who recognizes them, one obligation they chose, and one thing they changed? If not, add legibility and follow-through before adding more population or more regions.

## 14. Cultural Exchange in Ordinary Life — Draft

The history of exchange should be visible in ordinary routines, not confined to a codex. This section translates the route hypotheses in [`history.md` §XX](history.md#xx-cultural-exchange-atlas--route-hypotheses-not-canon) into everyday worldbuilding guidance. It introduces no settled migration, trade road, or cultural origin; those remain hypotheses until checked against the map and approved.

### What travels, and how it changes

- **Food:** ingredients move with seasonal work, trade, marriage, refuge and military supply. A dish becomes local when people adapt it to available crops, fuel, storage and household taste. Show the adaptation in the recipe, not a claim that one region “owns” every version.
- **Clothing and repair:** travelers carry useful cuts, fasteners, dyes and mending habits. Climate and labor change the silhouette. A garment's regional identity can be a detail—sleeve reinforcement, rain wrap, tool pocket—not a uniform worn by every resident.
- **Words and gestures:** guild phrases, counting calls, greetings and work songs travel easily. A phrase can keep its rhythm while its meaning shifts. The System can convey literal meaning while leaving register, humor or reference uncertain.
- **Tools and techniques:** techniques move through apprenticeships and demonstration. Record who taught whom when known; don't assume every shared shape proves borrowing. Local materials change the result.
- **Rites and memorials:** travelers may carry a personal gesture home, but communities decide whether to adopt it. Similar practices can have unrelated origins. A rite can be meaningful without being universally understood.
- **Stories:** a story changes with the audience. Guild versions emphasize risk and return; household versions may remember who was fed; an official version may credit a ruler. No version should automatically be treated as the only truthful one.

### Belonging can be layered

A person may identify with a birthplace, a clan, a trade, a household they joined, and the town where their children grew up. These ties can coexist or conflict. NPCs should not be forced into one label for every scene. A displaced family can preserve one custom, abandon another, and adapt a third without being portrayed as less authentic.

For a named character with a cross-region background, select only details that matter to the current scene. Their history should influence what they know, what they miss, whom they trust, or how they solve a problem—not become an endless checklist of cultural markers. Give them the right to disagree with common assumptions about their own community.

### Local knowledge is practical expertise

People know the world through work. A miller reads water height; a caravan cook notices a change in fuel; a guard recognizes a false guild seal; a shepherd sees when prey has changed its route; a shrine keeper knows which visitors need privacy. This knowledge should create concrete options in play. It does not make one occupation or culture inherently wiser than another.

Use this four-step pattern for a local custom or piece of knowledge:

1. The player first sees it being used in a practical moment.
2. A person explains only what they would naturally share.
3. The player can ask a follow-up, compare another account or simply move on.
4. Later, the knowledge helps in a small, readable way: a safer route, a more respectful greeting, a clue, a discount earned through trust, or a better interpretation of a rumor.

Do not make cultural fluency a hidden gate to basic services. Let curiosity improve context and relationships, while ordinary quest objectives remain understandable.

### A town's exchange profile

When developing a town, use a compact profile instead of adding a large collection of themed props:

| Prompt | Design answer |
|---|---|
| What arrives? | One or two imports that make geographic and economic sense once the route is verified. |
| What leaves? | A locally made good, service, skill or piece of knowledge. |
| Who carries it? | A visible role: porter, trader, student, pilgrim, guild party, seasonal worker or returning family. |
| What changed locally? | A recipe, tool, word, building detail or custom altered to fit local conditions. |
| Who benefits? | Name the workers and households, not only the institution or ruler. |
| What is contested? | A plausible tension over price, credit, access, sacred use, labor or historical attribution. |
| What can the player affect? | At most one compact state change in a small quest: service, rumor, display, relationship or journal record. |

This keeps towns distinct while allowing them to feel connected. Two places can share a product but differ in who makes it, who controls it, what it means and whether it is welcome.

### Dialogue and environmental guardrails

- Avoid using a real-world culture as a one-to-one template for an Aetherian people. Draw from multiple sources and transform the combination into a setting-specific practice.
- Do not make unfamiliar customs inherently sinister, comic or primitive. Their meaning should come from context and character.
- Let trade bring both opportunity and unequal power. A prosperous route can also create dangerous labor, extraction, debt or exclusion.
- Show variation inside each region: urban and rural, old and young, wealthy and poor, settled and traveling, devout and uncertain.
- Let characters correct the player's misunderstanding without humiliating them. Curiosity should be rewarded with context, not a quiz score.
- Keep the journal's phrasing careful: “the record suggests,” “locals remember,” “the Guild claims,” or “the origin remains disputed” when certainty is not earned.

### Compact implementation target

For the first playable region, one route hypothesis can be represented with a single imported object, one local adaptation, two NPC interpretations and one optional evidence interaction. The player may learn the story in a tavern, at a worksite or through a companion's question. No online service, player trading, global event or time-gated activity is required; this is authored, offline single-player content.

## 15. Recurring Town Life Without Calendar Chores — Draft

The locked seven-day week and 90-minute in-game day can make settlements feel cyclical. They should not make the player feel late for real life. Use time to change atmosphere, availability and small opportunities—not to delete quests, kill companions, permanently close a shop or advance a world-ending crisis while the player is elsewhere.

### A weekly rhythm players can learn

Give each town a few visible routines that recur predictably:

- a market day when more traveling vendors and gossip appear;
- a guild board refresh at dawn, with siege defence notices posted one day in advance where applicable;
- one local communal activity tied to the weekday or work cycle;
- a quiet period when workers go home, guards change shifts and the inn becomes a social hub;
- a weekly rest or observance that changes some dialogue and service hours without making the town inaccessible.

Only use the calendar facts already in Canon. The weekday names are settled; a universal year length, universal seasonal schedule or fixed festival calendar is not. Local observances should be described in relation to established weather, harvest, historical memory or community practice, and their dates should stay flexible until those systems are locked.

### Three horizons of time response

1. **Moment:** lights, weather, crowd density, work animations, ambient sound and who is visibly present shift during the day.
2. **Week:** boards refresh, recurring activities return, companions comment on the journey, and an inn or guild recognizes repeated visits.
3. **Story:** a choice or major quest changes settlement safety, reputation, visible repair, access or local dialogue. It changes only when the player or authored story causes it; it does not silently tick forward in real time.

This separation helps players understand why something changed. A market stall closing at night is a schedule. A guild suspension after contract abandonment is a consequence. A town's mood improving after liberation is story state. Do not present all three as opaque “world simulation.”

### Activity design: optional, repeatable, locally meaningful

Recurring activities should be a mix of observation, play and social contact: a small tournament bracket, a cooking demonstration, a public repair day, a storytelling circle, a shrine procession, a local game or a market appraisal. Each needs a clear opt-in and a short version. The player can watch, join, leave, or return next week. Rewards should be modest and never required for level or rank progression.

To avoid copy-paste repetition, vary one authored detail at a time: which NPC hosts, which rumor is discussed, what weather does to the activity, whether the town is besieged or liberated, or what the player's local reputation means to the audience. A reusable activity can acknowledge a major state without needing a complete alternate questline.

### Fail-forward timing rules

- Main-story choices remain available until their authored deadline is explicitly communicated in-world; no real-world clock is used.
- A player who chooses other content should encounter consequences as changed circumstances and response options, not as a surprise loss of all access.
- If a recurring activity is missed, it returns on its next cycle unless the story specifically and clearly changes it.
- A limited-time siege defence auto-resolves under Canon §4b when skipped; failure can reduce prosperity or damage repairable buildings but cannot destroy the settlement.
- Companion support is handled at the visible weekly or journey checkpoint defined in §11. No one dies or loses bond progress for a missed meal tick.
- Schedule-based shops must offer a readable alternative (another vendor, next opening time, or a board/inn service) for essential functions.

### Small-town implementation slice

For Brindlewick, prototype only three layers: a dawn board refresh, one weekly social activity, and a visible difference between Besieged and Liberated routines. Use existing NPCs and systems. Let the weekly activity surface one personal conversation, one local rumor and one optional low-stakes reward. Do not create new permanent NPCs or a new holiday date in this pass.

**Success check:** after missing one activity, the player knows when it returns, can still reach essential services, and sees a small believable change in the town. If the calendar produces anxiety or repetitive errands, simplify it.
