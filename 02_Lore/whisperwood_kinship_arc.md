# Whisperwood Kinship Arc — Draft

> **Design draft, not canon yet.** Built from the creator’s questionnaire answers. `Canon.md` wins on conflicts; this expands the existing Hollowpine, Whisperwood and Act I material without adding a region, settlement, Tyrant or ending.

## The emotional promise

Two strangers from peoples taught to fear one another survive their first night by choosing to protect each other. Their friendship does not erase history: it gives them a reason to investigate it, face its cost, and choose what they will do next.

The arc should feel like a complete anime season thread: an intimate first meeting, a growing mystery, a public rupture, a hard-won alliance and a spectacular defense that earns its hope. The player is never a prophesied savior here. Trust is built through playable decisions.

## The first meeting

For the **Transported** opening, the player wakes in Whisperwood at twilight. Mochi watches from the grass. A wounded, masked goblin scout is trying to hold off a monster attracted by a disturbed migration trail. The scout pulls the player out of the first strike; the player notices the monster’s tell and creates the opening that lets them fight together. Neither could have survived alone.

The Status System translates immediate meaning—*run*, *behind you*, *hurt*—but stumbles over names, humor and cultural meaning. The fight is fully playable. The scout is not a tutorial puppet: they can be downed, make mistakes and need the player’s help. Lyra finds the pair after the fight and gets the player to Brindlewick; the scout slips away, leaving a clan token behind.

This is an opening variation for the Transported origin, not a forced scene for every origin. Other origins can meet the scout naturally during their early Whisperwood contracts.

### Translation rules (draft)

The Status System is an interpreter, not a mind-reader. In early contact it turns common words into the most likely practical meaning, then exposes uncertainty through pauses, partial subtitles or an occasional correction. The precise confidence percentages in the dialogue samples are writer-side placeholders; a numeric accuracy meter is not required in the player HUD.

- It translates words and familiar grammar; it does not reveal a speaker's intent, honesty, memories or private thoughts.
- Proper names, clan titles, jokes, metaphors, legal phrases and old songs are unreliable at first. The System may give a literal rendering and leave the player to infer its social meaning.
- The translation's visual cue is consistent and accessible: a small glyph beside interpreted subtitles, optional sound cue, and a distinct style for uncertain words. Color alone never signals uncertainty.
- As the protagonist spends time with people and hears repeated words in context, the System's interpretation improves. Players can also learn phrases through optional conversations, journal entries and language lessons.
- This improvement is not a new leveling grind and never gates a required quest answer. The System provides enough practical translation for safety; cultural fluency comes from relationships.
- The System cannot certify whether a historical document is true. It can compare language and identify anachronisms, while evidence and testimony remain the player's work.

### Opening sequence, beat by beat

1. **Black screen, breath, rain.** The player hears an ordinary unfinished sentence from the blurred final day of their old life. No death flash or answer is shown.
2. **The first image.** A bead of rain hangs in the air, then drops upward for a moment as the Status System initializes. The camera opens to two moons through the trees. Mochi's reflection appears in a puddle before the slime itself comes into view.
3. **No tutorial wall.** The player can move, look, inspect and approach Mochi. The System gives one short prompt at a time; it labels what the character can sense and leaves the strange things unexplained.
4. **A sound from the dark.** A warning click, a trap-wire snapping, then the masked scout is thrown into the clearing by an existing Whisperwood monster. The scout raises an arm to keep the monster off the disoriented player. The first attack cue is readable through animation, sound and silhouette together.
5. **A shared opening.** The scout marks the monster and tries to trap a leg. The trap fails. The player sees the wind-up and chooses how to respond; their attack creates the moment the scout needs to recover. The scout draws the next lunge away from the player.
6. **The finishing exchange.** Neither character performs a super move. They improvise: the scout calls the direction, the player uses a tree root or rock as cover, and the monster exposes its weak side. The hit is decisive because the player read the encounter, not because the protagonist suddenly has overwhelming power.
7. **Quiet aftermath.** The scout reaches for a small knife when the player moves. The System mistranslates the first sentence as a threat; gestures and the scout's choice to lower the knife reveal the intended meaning. The two share water or a small ration before separating.
8. **Lyra's entrance.** Lyra arrives following the monster trail. Her first reaction is to put herself between the injured player and the goblin. She lowers her weapon after seeing the scout's wound and the monster's tracks. She takes the player toward Brindlewick; the scout leaves the six-mark token as a promise to meet again, not an invitation to trust blindly.

The camera can heighten key beats with inserts—a shaking hand, broken System text, one eye behind an amber lens, a sudden silence before the lunge—while the player stays in control during the fight. Sella's small musical motif begins as the two cooperate, builds into a full orchestral-anime swell at the finishing exchange, then returns as a quiet flute phrase once they are safe. The emotional contrast is deliberate: spectacular action, then room to breathe.

## The old border story

This is a draft framework, not a settled historical date. It connects to Hollowpine's established origin as six goblin clans who fled a northern war and settled in Whisperwood generations ago.

- **The goblin memory:** the refugees were promised access to a wooded route and its seasonal forage in exchange for keeping a monster corridor clear. Surface settlers later fenced the route, cut the marked trees and called the goblins trespassers.
- **The settlement memory:** goblin raiders attacked a supply caravan and burned a storehouse during a dangerous winter. The settlement claims the agreement ended after the attack and later barriers were built for safety.
- **The lived truth:** the caravan was attacked, but the first violence followed the closure of a route the goblins depended on. Some goblins wanted retaliation; some settlers wanted coexistence. Neither population acted as one.
- **The manipulation:** Azrael's agents changed dates, copied marks onto forged orders and circulated versions that emphasized the other side's worst acts. The forged documents are convincing because they build on real injuries and real people who made bad decisions.

The arc does not declare that the peoples are equally responsible for every harm. It distinguishes evidence from rumor, shows who profited from separation, and leaves room to condemn specific actions while rejecting inherited hatred.

### Where the clues live

The old account is rooted in places already established in Whisperwood:

- **The southern cart-road:** the player sees how the forest's shallow bowl channels caravans between Brindlewick and Millbrook. A fallen tree or broken bridge can close a route without looking like a political act at first.
- **The grey-stone waystations:** their stone does not occur in the wood. Bread left on their steps is an old local custom for travelers who never returned. The contrast between this quiet hospitality and the forged orders becomes a recurring image.
- **The Hush:** Mana Motes and glowcaps make the closed canopy beautiful and unnerving. The silence lets players hear the migration before they see it. It is also where the old road marker was moved.
- **The Rookie Stones:** tally marks show generations of guild field tests. One stone's lower carvings predate the guild's account of when the border route was closed.
- **The Millbrook stream:** spring runoff exposes a washed-out den and moves the prey corridor toward the farms. The waterwheel and mill give the defense a readable landmark and a believable reason for the town to depend on the road.
- **Hollowpine Camp:** the six clans' stronghold occupies a ruined waystation and a sinkhole cave. Sella can show the player how the same ruins serve as shelter, workshop and route marker; the location is not a generic enemy base.

Each location changes the story's mood. Brindlewick is warm but guarded; the road is uncertain; the Hush is wonder edged with fear; Hollowpine is busy, inventive and cramped; Millbrook is exposed and practical. Returning after liberation and after the patrol lets the player compare the same spaces under different levels of safety.

## Ecological context

The corridor is a practical piece of the world, not a metaphor that replaces ecology. Forest wolves hunt rabbits and boar; prey move toward safer forage; goblin mushroom plots and surface farms attract scavengers; the ley flicker makes monsters unusually sensitive to concentrated Aether. The creatures are displaced by changing water, food and den sites, with the flicker worsening an already stressed route.

Players can reason from visible signs before the migration event:

- fresh wolf tracks run against the usual wind and cross old prey trails;
- rabbit burrows are empty on one side of a stream and crowded on the other;
- mushroom beds have been stripped, but the spoor shows the gatherers were fleeing rather than feeding normally;
- a broken waystation marker has been moved, changing the route used by caravans and patrols;
- low-rank monsters appear in daylight because their usual shelter has been disturbed.

Killing migrating animals can protect people in immediate danger. It also changes the local population and may reduce later sightings, cores and materials. The arc avoids a perfect nonviolent answer: players defend people first, then can repair habitat and travel routes so the pressure is less likely to return. Monster cores remain valuable trade goods; the story does not shame players for using the established economy.

## The stranger: draft character

**Working name: Sella of the Wolfskin Clan** — an adult goblin scout from Hollowpine’s Wolfskin Clan. The name and role are provisional. Their bark-and-leather half-mask has amber lenses and a small six-mark clan motif; it hides their face from surface patrols and lets them work in smoke and spore-heavy tunnels. They are observant, dryly honest and more protective of their people than they are proud of being right.

Their people are not a single-minded enemy faction. Hollowpine’s six clans have distinct crafts and internal disagreements. Sella opposes raids on civilians, but does not believe the Guild’s account of the old border conflict either. They track wildlife and migration routes because a hungry or displaced monster can threaten goblin caves as readily as surface farms.

### Combat identity

If recruited on the earned-trust route, Sella is a **fast scout and tactical skirmisher**:

- marks a priority target and calls out its next attack;
- uses traps and short-range gadgets to interrupt or redirect enemies;
- creates openings for the player rather than competing for the final blow;
- retreats to cover when wounded and needs the player to protect them.

Their counterplay is readable through animation, sound, silhouette and color together. The player never needs to parry to benefit from Sella’s calls. They remain an AI character; the player does not switch control to them.

### Character direction

- **Silhouette:** short, forward-leaning goblin hunter; layered wolf-pelt mantle, narrow pack, visible rope, and a long-handled snare tool. The six-mark token is an asymmetrical piece of carved wood, not a military badge.
- **Mask:** bark-and-leather half-mask with amber lenses. It protects against smoke and spores and lets Sella pass unnoticed near surface patrols. It is functional cultural craft, not a permanent mystery box; Sella removes it in safe moments.
- **Palette:** soot charcoal, lichen green, warm amber and one red cord marking the Wolfskin Clan. Avoid coding goblins as uniformly dirty or evil; their color palette comes from forest materials and hunting camouflage.
- **Voice and movement:** clipped calls in battle, longer and more careful speech around civilians. Their ears and shoulders betray fear before their voice does. At first they stand where an exit remains visible; after trust grows, they sit with their back to a wall only when the player is nearby.
- **Private want:** Sella wants their clan to survive winter without raids becoming the only answer. Their fear is not that peace is impossible, but that the people who try it will leave their families exposed.
- **Contradiction:** they insist they are practical, yet keep a pocket ledger of animal tracks and names of people lost on the border. The ledger begins as field notes and gradually becomes a record of who suffered on both sides.
- **Humor:** restrained, situational and warm. The System's awkward translation occasionally turns a tactical phrase into something unintentionally grand; Sella corrects it later and insists they never said anything about “the glorious toe of destiny.”

### Bond scenes

1. **The token explained:** Sella identifies the six craft marks and admits the Guild's first assumption hurt more than they expected.
2. **A shared meal:** the player can try a goblin mushroom dish. Sella explains which ingredients are famine food and which are festival food; cooking is a social activity, not a compulsory buff chore.
3. **The old route:** the two follow tracks through a closed corridor. Sella tells the story they were taught, then asks what the player remembers from Earth—not as proof, but because the protagonist is also someone displaced from home.
4. **After the public confession:** Sella is not instantly reassured. The player can give them space, stay nearby or ask what practical help they need. Their response reflects the accumulated Trust and Respect flags.
5. **The dawn patrol:** Sella offers to join as a companion only after the provisional patrol has succeeded. If not recruited, they continue leading the local patrol and remain available for regional missions.

These scenes should be short, specific and interruptible. The relationship grows during play—walking, repairing gear, watching wildlife and sharing food—so intimacy is not limited to dialogue menus.

### Party skill progression

These are role concepts, not locked skill data. Keep Sella useful at low levels and avoid turning their support calls into mandatory parry prompts.

| Skill | Function | Anime presentation |
|---|---|---|
| **Trailmark** | Briefly marks a target's path and reveals the direction of its next committed attack | Amber line traces the target's movement; Sella calls a short warning |
| **Snareline** | A cable trap slows small enemies or briefly interrupts an elite after a clear setup | A bright cord snaps taut; the enemy's silhouette stays readable |
| **Smoke Seed** | Breaks enemy line of sight and gives the player room to reposition or revive an ally | A compact charcoal puff with amber flecks, never a screen-filling effect |
| **Split-Reed Step** | Sella darts through a marked opening and draws one enemy's attention | A quick low camera track and two clean impact frames, then control returns |
| **Six-Mark Counter** | Once per major encounter, Sella coordinates nearby allies after the player creates an opening | A brief group call-and-response, then a playable team attack rather than a long cutscene |

The visual language grows as the relationship grows: early calls are practical and isolated; later they are understood instantly, with party banter and matching movement. No move grants invulnerability, deletes a boss phase or makes Sella the protagonist.

## Fit with the Goblin Chief and regional siege

Sella is not secretly plotting Grukk's death. Grukk is their chief and has kept Hollowpine's six clans fed; they also know that raids hurt people who had little say in the old border conflict. They want the raids to stop without their families being left hungry or exposed.

The existing F-to-E trial against Grukk remains the decisive Tyrant encounter and liberation event. Sella can disagree with the player, ask whether they understand the cost to Hollowpine, and still help protect civilians during the siege. The game must not offer a false promise that a speech can bypass the boss or the rank trial. Before the fight, the player can gather supplies, document Grukk's raids, protect goblin noncombatants and secure a path for the six clans to reorganize afterward.

After Grukk falls, Sella's response reflects the player's conduct:

- protecting goblin civilians and keeping the rank trial fair earns respect, even if Sella grieves;
- humiliating prisoners, looting homes or breaking a supply promise can turn them into a rival;
- helping the clans distribute food and secure the migration corridor begins repair;
- killing the Tyrant does not make every goblin hostile or make the raided settlements instantly safe in every respect.

The region becomes Liberated according to existing rules. Its siege attacks stop, ordinary contracts return and the festival can include a quiet, complicated memorial as well as celebration. The story lets happiness and grief share the same scene.

## Timeline and pacing integration

| Story window | What the player sees | What stays unresolved |
|---|---|---|
| **Transported prologue** | Mutual first fight, broken translation, token, Lyra's arrival | Sella's name, old border history, why the monster moved |
| **Early Act I** | Token recognition, missing-cart contract, rumors, first migration signs | Whether records were forged; Sella's position on Grukk |
| **Act I rank trial** | Grukk's raids and the Tyrant fight; the player can prepare for post-fight civilian needs | Who shaped the old story and whether the clans can reconcile |
| **Post-liberation / Act II** | Evidence chain, public truth, guarded summit, opportunity to prevent an attack | How deep Azrael's network reaches elsewhere |
| **Regional aftermath** | Joint patrol and defense against a migration surge | Broader ecosystem recovery; later clan diplomacy |

This pacing keeps the opening focused. The player is not asked to solve a generations-old conspiracy before learning the controls, meeting Lyra or understanding the Guild. Early scenes make the mystery personal; later scenes let the player investigate it properly.

## Scene and dialogue sketches

These are tone samples, not final script. The System's translation should feel useful and slightly uncanny, never like a narrator explaining the lore.

### First exchange after the fight

**System:** `Nearby speech detected. Translation: “You stepped where the ground was listening.” Confidence: 41%.`

**Scout:** “It means the roots were moving.”

**System:** `Correction accepted. Confidence: 63%.`

The scout offers a strip of clean cloth. If the player accepts, they wrap the scout's arm first; if the player uses it on themself, the scout notices but does not punish the choice. Lyra can later ask why the player trusted a goblin. The response options express curiosity, caution or refusal to judge from appearance; none changes the fact that Lyra escorts the player to safety.

### The token recognized

**Guild clerk:** “Six marks. Hollowpine. That means raider.”

**Sella:** “It means six families made this. The raiders use one mark.”

**Player:** the player can ask what each mark means, ask why Sella hid it, or challenge the clerk's conclusion.

**Sella:** “If I show the whole mark, you decide you know me. If I hide it, you decide I’m guilty. Tell me which choice was safer.”

The scene should not end with a perfect comeback. The clerk has seen raids and is afraid; Sella has seen goblin civilians blamed for them. The tension is real on both sides.

### Before Grukk's trial

Sella watches the player prepare. They do not order the player to spare Grukk; that would contradict the mandatory rank trial and regional siege. Instead they ask what the player intends to do for the people left behind.

**Sella:** “You can stop the raids. You can't make six families stop being hungry by winning one fight.”

The player can promise to help secure food, decline to promise, or say the Guild's duty ends at the trial. The game records honesty and follow-through separately. A broken promise matters more than choosing the blunt answer.

### Public reveal

The presentation is staged in a public gathering where both sides can challenge the evidence. The camera stays on listeners while the player speaks; no long hero monologue replaces player choice. When the crowd turns, Sella speaks from among them, not from a raised platform. Their confession is followed by silence, then specific objections from people who lost relatives. The scene only shifts when the player answers those objections with evidence and accountability.

### Dawn after the patrol

Sella returns the player's clan token—not as a symbol that the conflict is solved, but because it has become a shared keepsake. They offer a place on the next patrol. If recruited, they add: “You take first watch. I trust your eyes. I do not trust your sense of direction.” This is the first light comedy after the heavier summit, built from established characterization rather than a gag interruption.

### The cart driver and the goblin guard

**Driver:** “They took the flour. I saw the mask.”

**Sella:** “You saw a mask. Did you see who carried it?”

**Driver:** “I saw the wheel break. Then I saw your knife.”

The player can stop the argument, ask both to describe only what they observed, or let Sella question the driver. This makes the gap between observation and conclusion a playable habit, not a lecture about prejudice.

### The song and the seal

**Elder:** “That verse is older than the road.”

**System:** `Literal translation: “When six marks shared the crossing, no one went hungry.” Idiom uncertain.`

**Sella:** “It doesn't promise peace. It promises passage.”

The line helps players understand that history can hold a practical agreement without proving that everyone loved each other. It also lets Sella interpret their own tradition rather than positioning the System as the cultural authority.

### The first shared patrol

**Guard:** “Which side of the stream do we take?”

**Sella:** “The side where the ground hasn't moved.”

**Player:** choose to ask why, trust Sella's judgment or check the map first.

**Sella:** “Good. Ask the ground too.”

This is a small, warm beat before the migration clue. The player's choice affects the route's preparation but does not determine whether the migration exists.

### The tracks turn

**Sella:** “Wolves and rabbits. Same direction.”

**System:** `Predator-prey co-migration: atypical. Cause unknown.`

**Sella:** “They're not chasing each other. They're running from the same thing.”

The player can inspect the stream, climb the ridge or signal the settlements immediately. The line establishes urgency, while the investigation remains playable.

### The defense signal

**Sella:** “Lanterns up! Keep the road clear!”

**Guard captain:** “And the farms?”

**Sella:** “I have them. Trust the line!”

**Sella, after the player opens the counterattack:** “Now. Together!”

The shouted language is short enough to survive combat noise and subtitle timing. The heroic beat belongs to the group response; the player still aims, moves and lands the decisive action.

### After the road reopens

**Sella:** “The old map ended here.”

**Player:** choose to ask what comes next, offer to walk with them or say goodbye for now.

**Sella:** “Then we draw the next part.”

This closes the arc's central image: a map inherited as a border becomes a map the characters make together. It leaves room for future discovery and does not claim that the clans' politics are settled forever.

## Choice and consequence matrix

| Player behavior | Immediate response | Later consequence | Repair path |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shares evidence with both sides | Some distrust, better preparation | More leaders attend the summit | Continue gathering corroboration |
| Reports everything only to the Guild | Guild trust rises; goblin trust falls | Public reveal requires an additional witness | Show the original sources publicly |
| Keeps evidence private to protect one clan | Short-term calm | The other side suspects a cover-up | Disclose the reason and accept scrutiny |
| Protects civilians during the siege | Injuries and damage are reduced | Sella's respect rises; the joint patrol starts earlier | Continue helping recovery |
| Ignores civilian danger to pursue a target | The target may be captured sooner | Both clans remember who was left exposed | Return supplies, help the injured, admit the choice |
| Uses force against armed raiders | Immediate threat falls | No automatic hostility toward all goblins | Distinguish combatants from civilians in later choices |
| Kills wildlife outside the defense | Materials and cores are gained | Local sightings and migration pressure change | Restore habitat or protect the remaining route |
| Prevents the summit attack | The meeting remains intact | Leaders credit preparation, not luck | Maintain the patrol and share intelligence |
| Fails to prevent the attack | Playable defense and repair costs | Trust is shaken; no settlement is destroyed | Rescue, repair, and continue the summit |

There is no hidden “perfect peace” meter. The intended outcome is not that every character agrees. The arc succeeds when the player uncovers what can be proven, keeps people alive, and creates a process where future disputes can be handled without inherited falsehood deciding the answer.

Consequences arrive on three readable clocks: **immediate** reactions (rumors, arguments, companion responses, a public reprimand after a visible failure); **near-term** changes over the next few in-game days (guards, prices, repairs, patrols and schedules); and **regional** changes within roughly one in-game month (restored routes, safer travel, joint contracts and a calmer settlement look). The player sees progress before the month ends, and every damage state in this arc has a recovery path.

## Clan texture: the Wolfskin within Hollowpine

The Wolfskin Clan is one of six clans, not a separate goblin nation. Its hunters know the animal trails, but they are not all warriors: some tan hides, raise young, map safe routes, trade pelts, or negotiate access to the mushroom farms. Sella comes from a family that maps routes and trains young hunters to leave prey corridors open during breeding season.

Their practical customs:

- A hunter ties one cord to a pack for each person they are responsible for bringing home; losing a cord is treated seriously.
- Before a long hunt, the group leaves a portion of dried food at the den entrance for anyone returning late. This is an ordinary safety practice, not a magical ritual.
- A track is described by direction, weather and urgency before species; inexperienced hunters who name it first are teased for “letting the story run ahead of the pawprint.”
- The mask's amber lens is exchanged between family members when someone takes over a watch. Returning it means the watch is done and the next person accepts responsibility.

These customs give quests and banter concrete objects to use. They are not universal goblin rules; the other five clans keep their own practices, and individual goblins may reject or reinterpret them.

## Anime presentation guide for this arc

- **Opening:** a bold, character-led anime opening after character creation. The protagonist appears in the player's chosen design, origin outfit and weapon/class silhouette; the sequence races from the two moons to the first fight, then flashes through clan emblems, regional landscapes, companions and a glimpse of the migration. The montage uses original characters and visual motifs, not copied shots or recognizable franchise designs. It teases wonder and danger without spoiling later reveals.
- **Action:** clear anticipation, one sharp impact pose, readable recovery, then player control. Use speed lines and screen shake as brief accents; maintain the boss/monster's visible shape and telegraph.
- **Faces:** prioritize eyes, hands and posture during trust scenes. The masked scout's eye and ear movement carry emotion before dialogue explains it.
- **Color:** amber lanterns and warm food scenes build safety; forest greens turn blue-gray when the migration route is empty; red cult marks appear only as small, repeatable clues before the reveal.
- **Music:** a flute-and-plucked-string phrase belongs to Sella; it begins as scattered notes, becomes a rhythmic patrol motif and joins the regional orchestration at the counterattack. The opening can grow into a full anime-rock/J-pop chorus for the decisive playable moment. Keep the largest boss arrangement reserved for the existing Tyrant battle and later major set pieces.
- **Monologues:** use brief internal observations at chapter turns, especially the protagonist comparing the unfamiliar world with old Earth. Do not interrupt controls during ordinary encounters. The player's inner voice should sound curious, vulnerable and hopeful—not like a tactical guide.
- **Comedy:** a little dry warmth after tension, never at the expense of injured civilians, grief or goblin culture.

## Order flexibility and continuity cases

The arc has authored emotional milestones, but players should be able to approach evidence, travel and combat in different orders. The following cases prevent one missed prompt from collapsing a long story thread.

| Player path | Required response from the story | What must remain true |
|---|---|---|
| The player loses the opening fight | Short retry with a clearer telegraph and Sella drawing the monster away | No penalty, no changed relationship state, no permanent loss |
| The player leaves without taking the token | Sella leaves a second mark at the Oaken Card later, or the Guild identifies it from an existing contract record | The player can still meet Sella and discover the clan connection |
| The player never asks about the token | The cart contract naturally brings Sella back; the token is recognized during the rescue | Main story does not require the player to interrogate every townsperson |
| The player skips one clue source | Another independent source can establish the fact | Evidence requires corroboration, not a particular class or Unique Skill |
| The player reports only to the Guild | Guild investigators preserve one record while Sella withholds trust | The public reveal needs an extra witness or a later proof; the arc stays completable |
| The player tells Hollowpine first | Clan delegates protect the song and route marker; settlement trust falls temporarily | The player can later disclose the whole source chain and rebuild trust |
| The player defeats Grukk before meeting Sella | Sella's first direct scene shifts to the post-trial aftermath | The relationship begins with grief and accountability, not a retconned pre-boss friendship |
| The player defeats Grukk after meeting Sella | The pre-trial promise and player conduct inform the aftermath | Sella's conflict is acknowledged; no speech bypasses the mandatory trial |
| Sella is a rival or enemy at the summit | Another existing NPC can lead the patrol and defense; Sella may arrive independently if the evidence convinces them | Sella never becomes a required party slot for story completion |
| The player travels away before the Stampede | The story event waits for the player and is re-announced on the board/rumor system | No off-screen destruction, hidden time limit or forced failure |
| The player misses all preparation | The defense begins with a harder first phase and repair costs | Survivors, questline and settlement remain recoverable |
| The player returns after many in-game weeks | NPCs describe the actual recovery state and whether the pact held | No “urgent” dialogue is played as if no time passed |
| The player changes class or difficulty | Investigation methods and the defense's minimum success path remain available | No required parry, class skill or single-build counter |
| The player plays a non-Transported origin | Sella appears through an early contract, not as a forced duplicate prologue | The relationship works without pretending every origin woke in the same clearing |

### Continuity rules for dialogue

- Characters use the player's chosen name consistently in subtitles, journals and spoken lines where supported. If voice production is limited, names can be avoided naturally in audio while remaining visible in text.
- Sella remembers actions with concrete references: “you brought the storehouse food,” “you reported the token,” or “you left the driver behind.” Avoid generic lines that claim the relationship is good or bad without naming why.
- Other companions give one reaction per major beat, then allow the player to continue. Party banter is contextual and rate-limited so the same opinion does not repeat on every visit.
- If the player has never met a supporting cast member, dialogue refers to their role rather than assuming a relationship. If that character is dead or absent under a chosen difficulty/story outcome, a living substitute from the already-established local cast can perform the practical task.
- A failed or abandoned contract raises Guild concern and may affect trust or payment according to the existing contract rules. It does not secretly erase evidence already found or permanently close the kinship arc.

### Failure states and recovery design

There are three kinds of failure here. **Encounter failure** is a retry or a safer route. **Social failure** means a character distrusts the player, a meeting becomes harder, or a contract pays less; the player can repair it through a clear act. **Regional failure** means injuries, damaged stock or slower Prosperity recovery. It creates visible work and changed routines, while settlements and core story access remain intact.

The game should show recovery requirements directly: an NPC asks for a stated number of supplies, the map marks the damaged waystation, and the Journal says what evidence remains missing. Avoid invisible timers that quietly make recovery impossible. World reactions can be stern; the player should understand what happened and have a reasonable next step.

## Sella's personal questline (draft)

The arc's regional plot is about public history; Sella's personal quests are about the future they can choose for themself. These can run alongside the main arc and should not require the player to complete every one to understand the migration.

1. **A Map with Six Edges** — Sella asks the player to carry a new route map through all six Hollowpine craft areas. Each clan corrects one part of it. The player sees differences among the clans and learns that Sella's original map deliberately omitted a dangerous shortcut used by raiders.
2. **The Name on the Snare** — A hunter's trap injures a goblin child and a rabbit-kin courier. Sella initially blames the surface patrol; evidence shows the snare was made from a recycled Goblin Trapper device. The player can help identify the maker, repair the trap or arrange compensation. This confronts Sella's own bias without equating accidental injury with deliberate violence.
3. **The Storehouse Ledger** — With winter supplies low, Sella asks the player to audit the route and supply list with Fizzwick. The player can secure food through trade, gather, or negotiate shared storage with Millbrook. The quest demonstrates that practical peace requires logistics, and that dependence can feel frightening to both sides.
4. **A Trail Left Open** — Sella and the player lead a mixed patrol along a restored migration corridor. The player chooses whether to hunt one dangerous predator, redirect it, or mark a safe path around its den. The chosen approach changes local sightings and materials over the next few weeks.
5. **The Sixth Lantern** — After the summit, Sella is invited to place a lantern at a shared waystation. The ceremony is not a marriage of cultures or a political surrender; it is a promise that travelers from either side can ask for help. Sella can accept, refuse, or ask the player to stand beside them. The choice resolves whether they become a friend, rival or remain an independent ally.

If Sella becomes a companion, these quests add meaningful party context but do not multiply into a full romance route automatically. The approved companion roster and romance decisions in Canon remain authoritative.

## AI companion behavior

Sella should feel like an independent scout, not a second player avatar:

- **Free Rein:** chooses targets based on immediate threat, marks dangerous wind-ups, uses traps on clustered enemies and withdraws when critically wounded.
- **Command wheel:** the player can ask Sella to focus a target, hold position, protect a civilian or reserve gadgets. A command is a request; Sella still responds to danger and their personality.
- **No character swapping:** Sella's animations, skills and AI are visible to the player, but the player always controls their own character.
- **Trust response:** after a promise is broken, Sella may stop offering optional risk-taking skills and ask the player directly about it at camp. There is warning and a chance to repair; no silent overnight departure.
- **Accessibility:** callouts have captions and distinct sound plus animation cues; color is never the only signal. Players can lower callout frequency, disable camera emphasis and use larger subtitles.
- **Combat fairness:** Sella cannot be the only source of a boss opening, investigation clue or required counter. The player can complete the story and bosses without recruiting them or using parry.

## Region follow-through

The provisional patrol can feed the existing weekly board without inventing a second board system. After the story, normal hunting contracts remain available. Occasional joint-patrol contracts can rotate through the ordinary pools: escort a trader, gather winter forage, investigate a displaced nest, or clear a dangerous route. They are optional and never replace the week's Day Special or a scheduled siege defense.

Changes appear in layers: a paired guard post at a road marker, new produce or repaired tools in the market, a different rumor at the inn, then a rebuilt waystation once Prosperity recovers. The player does not place buildings or decorate the settlement; their work changes what the people can safely maintain.

The Memory Reel can capture the first shared fight, the public confession, the defended evacuation lane and the dawn patrol. Players may retake or remove optional photos; no upsetting scene is forced into a permanent album. The ending can show those images among other chosen journey moments without declaring that this one friendship defines every player's story.

## The arc’s progression

### 1. A token in the guild hall

After the opening, the player can follow the token through ordinary contracts. Sella reappears during a search for a missing supply cart. They disagree about what happened but cooperate to rescue its driver. This starts a relationship tracked by actions: keeping a promise, sharing evidence, respecting goblin civilians and how the player handles dangerous monsters.

The scout does not immediately announce their full name or clan. The token first proves only that they belong to a group with six marks. A guild clerk calls it a raider's insignia; a merchant recognizes one mark from a legal trade bundle; Sella later explains that it identifies a family craft, not a warband. The player learns the culture in pieces as trust grows.

### 2. Two inherited histories

Guild and goblin accounts tell incompatible versions of an old border agreement. Each side remembers a real harm; neither account explains the full sequence. Clues are found through tracking, old route markers, damaged records and testimony—not a single exposition dump.

The investigation has several valid methods. A tracker reads spoor and weathering; a scholar compares ink and dates; a social player asks elders to tell the story without accusing them; a crafter restores a broken marker; a stealth-oriented player retrieves a ledger held by an agent. Appraisal may identify material and age but never prints moral truth as a solved answer.

### 3. Signs on the old road

Before the summit, the player notices isolated ecological clues but does not yet see the full migration. A missing cart has been moved off the road by its driver; a rabbit trail abruptly ends at a washed-out bank; the goblin mushroom stores are being raided by animals that normally forage farther north. Each incident can be explained away on its own. Together, they point to a route under pressure.

This is foreshadowing and an optional investigation trail, not the full Stampede. The player can record tracks, question travelers or move supplies away from the threatened route. Those preparations will help later, but the story does not punish a player who has not assembled the whole pattern yet.

The evidence has a physical chain: changing water and forage move prey; predators follow; a broken waystation marker funnels both toward settlements. The ley flicker intensifies the animals' response to Aether but does not magically cause the entire migration by itself.

### 4. The forged history

The evidence points to agents of **Demon Overlord Azrael** altering records and spreading tailored stories to keep the clans divided. This is a draft link to the established villain, not a new faction or new Demon General. Azrael’s agents exploited old wounds; they did not invent every grievance or make either people blameless.

The clues are fair on a second viewing: a shared phrase preserved in two songs, a date that cannot fit both records, and the same mark under two supposedly unrelated seals. The Status System can translate the songs but cannot decide which speaker is telling the truth.

The evidence chain should require at least two independent clues, but never one specific class or skill. A report names a courier who carried a forged order; an archive copy predates the supposed original; a clan song preserves the route's older name. The player can expose the forgery after missing one clue if they find another source. The journal separates **confirmed**, **contradicted** and **unverified** statements so deduction stays legible.

### 5. The public reveal and summit

The player chooses to present the evidence publicly. The crowd reacts with anger before it reacts with hope. Sella admits they were afraid to believe the player and that fear helped preserve the lie, then says:

> “I was taught to fear your people. I was afraid the stories were true. But I won’t let that fear decide what’s true now.”

The clans arrange a guarded leaders’ meeting. If the player investigates the agents’ plan and prepares the defenses, they can prevent an attack before it starts. If they miss the clues, an attack becomes a playable emergency: losses and damage are repairable, and the meeting can continue. No clan or settlement is permanently destroyed.

The public scene should not force one speech onto every personality or origin. The player chooses which evidence to present and whether to acknowledge harm caused by their own side. The System supplies a quiet objective—*make them listen*—not a dialogue solution. Sella's confession is the emotional center, but the player's response determines whether the audience hears a plea for unity, a demand for accountability or both.

At the summit, the leaders do not sign a permanent alliance. They agree to a provisional patrol and a shared inquiry into the migration. That limited pact is credible, gives both sides a way to test cooperation and leaves room for later politics.

### 6. The first joint patrol

After the summit, members of both peoples volunteer for a joint patrol. They do not become instant friends; they share a dangerous road and watch one another’s backs. The patrol becomes a recurring regional activity that can reveal future migrations, missing travelers and signs of Azrael’s interference.

Sella leads the first counterattack in a set piece where each group uses its strengths: goblin tinkerers deploy a smoke curtain, hunters guide civilians, and settlement guards hold a narrow crossing. Sella calls the timing, then trusts the player to make the decisive opening. The camera briefly widens to show the line moving together, then returns to player control for the playable finish.

The aftermath is deliberately small: a goblin child offers a human guard a mushroom bun; the guard hesitates, then accepts. Sella watches rather than narrates. The next joint patrol begins at dawn, with awkward introductions and practical arguments about who carries the map. Hope is shown as people doing the next difficult task together.

### 7. The migration defense

On the first patrol, the two groups discover the full migration: forest wolves and prey species are crossing the old border together because their den and forage routes have shifted. They are not attacking out of malice. They are frightened, hungry animals moving through a corridor that no longer has room for them.

The patrol spots the wave before it reaches the settlements. The player chooses the defense plan and can draw on earlier preparation: warn households, open the evacuation lane, place traps that redirect rather than maim, repair a barricade, hire guards, or assign Sella to mark the fastest-moving threat. The player is not forced to choose between protecting people and caring about ecology; the immediate defense comes first, then the cause can be addressed.

The combat is a 5–8 minute story set piece, built in readable phases:

1. **The warning:** a scout signal crosses the ridge. The camera shows the scale of the migration, then returns control as the player reaches the first roadblock.
2. **The evacuation:** civilians follow lanterns to a protected lane. The player can clear enemies, carry a child or turn a frightened animal away from the route.
3. **The broken line:** a wave tests crowd control and a larger animal breaks through the weak barricade. Sella marks its path; the player chooses to stagger it, bait it, use an element or take a safer route around it.
4. **The split:** one flank approaches the farms while another crosses toward the goblin stores. The player chooses which to hold. Sella leads the other side, with companions and NPC adventurers helping according to prior relationships.
5. **The counterattack:** Sella calls the coordinated signal. The player creates the opening and leads the finishing push. The scene gets anime-scale framing and a music lift, but the player performs the decisive action.
6. **The quiet:** the last animals escape into the dark beyond the settlement. The clans count the injured and check who made it home before anyone celebrates.

This is a one-time story Stampede after Whisperwood is Liberated, not an additional recurring siege. If it collides with a scheduled event, the event calendar moves it to a clear day; it never stacks with a weekly defense or steals the board's top contract.

The defense saves people but does not solve the ecological cause. Afterward, the player can clear debris, restore the corridor, relocate forage plots, post seasonal warnings or keep hunting the dangerous animals. Each approach has tradeoffs in safety, prosperity, sightings and drops. No approach can permanently destroy a settlement or make the region unrecoverable.

The emotional payoff is Sella leading both clans through the counterattack. Their calls name the actions people can take—“hold the lantern line,” “leave the den open,” “now, together”—rather than delivering a speech. A brief wide shot shows the whole group moving in concert; the player immediately returns to control to finish the encounter.

## Playable node dossiers

### Node 1 — Two Moons, One Fight

**Purpose:** make the new world wondrous, make the protagonist vulnerable and establish that cooperation is the first source of strength.

**Entry:** the Transported character wakes in the rain. The playable opening gives them time to look around before combat. Mochi is curious, not a combat threat. The System's first message is useful but strange, and one line glitches about the protagonist's soul origin. The masked scout is already wounded when the existing forest monster drives them into the clearing.

**Player verbs:** look, move, interact, dodge or block, strike, use terrain, choose lock-on or free aim. The opening does not require parry, a specific class or one weapon type. On the first telegraph, Sella's body language and the monster's movement reinforce the sound and status cue. The System describes what is happening in simple language but does not tell the player the exact button to press after the initial controls have been taught.

**Success and failure:** the encounter is tuned for a level-one character and allows recovery. If the player falls, Sella drags them behind a root and the monster's attention shifts; this is a safety net, not an alternate victory cutscene. A second failure restarts the short encounter without a story penalty. Sella can be downed too, so the player learns that assistance is mutual.

**Emotional result:** each person takes a risk for the other. The reward is not a power-up but the token and a fragile reason to meet again. The scene ends with rain, breathing and the theme's first two notes rather than a triumphant fanfare.

### Node 2 — Six Marks

**Purpose:** let the player discover that the symbol is cultural information, not a morality label.

**Entry:** the player can ask Mina, a trader, an inn worker or a guard about the token. Each gives a partial answer shaped by their job and experience. No character is assigned as “the prejudiced one” while everyone else is enlightened: the guard remembers the attacks, the trader remembers lawful trade, and Mina is cautious about turning a rumor into a Guild record.

**Play:** revisit the Oaken Card, the market and the Sleeping Griffon; compare what people call the token; identify the six marks in the proper order. A journal entry records the sources and notes disagreement. Returning the token to Sella can be done honestly, through a neutral intermediary or after hiding it.

**Choices:** ask Sella directly, test the Guild's claim, or avoid the issue. The player can say they trust Sella, reserve judgment or distrust them. The game tracks the reason through behavior, not a morality response.

**Result:** Sella explains the mark as six crafts joined in one refugee camp. They correct the assumption that all goblins follow the warband and warn that the six-clan arrangement can break under pressure. A new rumor appears on the inn board about an old road cart that never arrived.

### Node 3 — The Cart on the Old Road

**Purpose:** turn a personal meeting into a practical partnership and give exploration, investigation and combat equal room.

**Entry:** a routine Guild contract asks the player to locate a missing supply cart. The surface report says goblins attacked it; the route has signs of a struggle, but the cart tracks leave the road before the goblin bootprints appear. Sella is already following the trail from the other end.

**Play:** follow wheel ruts, inspect snapped branches, listen for a stuck animal, and decide whether to approach the old waystation by the road, through the streambed or along a rocky ridge. A class with tracking tools gets clearer clues; other classes can ask the driver, inspect disturbed soil or use a flare to reveal the route. The encounter can be solved with a short fight, a trap, stealth or luring the monster away.

**Choice:** when an armed goblin is found guarding the trapped driver, the player can attack, call Sella to negotiate, or try to separate both sides. The goblin is frightened and wants the cart's winter food, not a trophy. The driver still has a legitimate need for the supplies.

**Result:** rescue both without forcing a perfect resolution. The player chooses how to divide or replace the food; the Guild pays the contract either way. Sella's trust increases most when the player is honest about what they took or promised. The scene yields the first account of a former border agreement.

### Node 4 — The Song and the Seal

**Purpose:** make deduction feel like investigation the player performs, not a cutscene that gives away the answer.

**Entry:** after the cart event, Sella and the player compare three sources: the Guild's copied route order, a verse preserved by one Hollowpine clan and a physical marker at the old waystation. The sources disagree about who closed the route and when.

**Play:** inspect ink, paper, fibers and seal impressions; compare the song's repeated phrase with the marked stones; ask elders what they remember but let them decline. Optional skills can make an approach faster or reveal a hidden source. No Unique Skill is the only solution. The clue board separates facts from interpretations and lets the player pin a hypothesis without being graded on it.

**Choice:** report the evidence to the Guild first, share it with both sides, or keep searching. Reporting first may protect records from destruction but makes Hollowpine fear another official cover-up. Public sharing builds trust more slowly and may attract the agents' attention.

**Result:** enough independent sources prove that someone altered the history, but not yet who. A date mismatch and a copied mark point to an external hand. The System confirms the age of the paper and its translation, then explicitly marks motive as unknown.

### Node 5 — Words Before Weapons

**Purpose:** pay off the investigation with public accountability and make the summit a political scene the player has earned.

**Entry:** the player calls a gathering at a current settlement site already present in the world data. The meeting includes a Guild representative, Hollowpine delegates, affected families and Sella. No new settlement is required. Both sides enter with guards and may leave at any time.

**Play:** select which two or more pieces of evidence to present; choose the order; acknowledge or omit harm committed by the player's own side. The scene uses dialogue options for tone and factual choices for content. Sella's confession follows the crowd's first angry reaction, not the other way around, so it feels costly rather than rehearsed.

**Attack preparation:** before the meeting, optional investigation can uncover an agent's signal route and intended timing. Players can move people to safety, check exits, place a lookout or quietly ask both guard units to stand together. The summit attack is preventable if the player connects clues and prepares. If it happens, the player protects people in a short, contained combat sequence; no clan leader or settlement is permanently killed or erased.

The intended sabotage is a false-flag attempt: an operative plans to damage the evidence stand, trigger a lantern fire and leave a goblin-made snare near a human guard. The sequence is designed to make each side blame the other while the primary documents burn. Preparation can include checking the lantern oil, comparing the snare knot with the evidence already found, guarding the archive case or changing the meeting layout. No one clue is mandatory; any two credible checks reveal the pattern.

If the player prevents the attempt, it is a tense stealth/social scene with a short intervention, not an assassination. If the attack begins, the objective is to protect people, preserve the records and expose the staged evidence. Capturing or disarming the operative is possible, but killing them is not required. If the primary documents are damaged, the second independent sources remain sufficient to prove the forgery.

**Result:** the meeting ends in a provisional inquiry and joint patrol, not instant friendship. Sella chooses to publicly defend the protagonist, risking standing with their own clan. Later rumors may describe the speech differently, giving the player a reason to revisit the town and see which version caught on.

### Node 6 — First Patrol at Dawn

**Purpose:** make reconciliation visible through ordinary shared work.

**Entry:** at sunrise, volunteers from both peoples gather for a patrol along the reopened road. The player can join immediately or finish preparations first. Sella is among the first to arrive, but does not assume that everyone will trust them.

**Play:** assign the group to inspect the road, carry supplies, test the lanterns or map the stream crossing. The command wheel offers simple requests for Sella during the patrol—scout ahead, hold, mark danger or assist a civilian. Sella can challenge a command if it endangers civilians or ignores their knowledge, then offers a workable alternative.

**Discovery:** the group finds signs of a larger movement: predator tracks crossing prey trails, damaged burrows, and a watercourse shifted by recent ground movement. This is the first time the pattern becomes undeniable. Sella's notes and the player's earlier evidence combine; if the player missed the clues, Sella catches them up through field observations rather than a lore lecture.

**Result:** a lookout sees the migration approaching the inhabited routes. The group has minutes to warn people. The player makes a quick, practical decision about the evacuation route, beginning the defense node.

### Node 7 — Tracks That Turned

**Purpose:** give the player the final readable evidence that the ecological crisis is real, and let investigation shape the defense.

**Observation:** the player follows tracks from the stream to the Hush and compares them with their earlier field notes. They see predator and prey moving together, which indicates panic rather than ordinary hunting. The shifted stream, closed forage route and moved marker form a causal chain the player can explain to the patrol.

**Choice:** decide whether to warn both settlements immediately, take time to move stores and prepare traps, or send a runner to confirm the route. Fast action gives less preparation but more warning time; a careful check improves defense placement but leaves fewer minutes before the first wave reaches the road.

**Result:** both clans receive the warning. The player now knows where the animals are likely to cross and can choose where to prepare the defense. The story clearly communicates uncertainty and stakes: the migration is approaching, but its exact route depends on wind, water and the player’s diversion work.

### Node 8 — A Wall of Lanterns

**Purpose:** resolve the immediate danger with a large playable anime set piece that tests adaptation and teamwork rather than parry mastery.

**Preparation:** depending on earlier choices, the player has more or less time to set barricades, place redirecting traps, move food stores, hire guards, send a runner or ask companions/NPC adventurers to hold a flank. All actions use existing siege preparation and prosperity systems. The migration happens after liberation, so it is a rare story Stampede rather than another recurring siege.

**Combat:** the opening wave is composed of common regional monsters acting in panic. The second wave includes faster predators and a larger animal whose tells are learnable. The player may lock on, free aim, use elements, interrupt, dodge or use a crafted tool. The defense communicates target priority through icons, voice lines, animation and audio, with color as a secondary cue.

**Player decision:** choose to hold the farm road or goblin stores. The chosen side gets the player directly; Sella leads the other side with a small AI squad. Earlier trust changes coordination: high trust gives quicker callouts and better guard placement; low trust still permits success but leaves the player to solve more of the fight alone.

**Anime payoff:** once the player creates an opening, Sella leads the combined counterattack. Use one music lift, expressive group animation and a brief wide shot, then return to player control for the finisher. No forced ultimate or player power-up is needed.

**Outcome:** stronger preparation reduces wounds and repair cost. Failure causes visible loss of stock and prosperity, followed by an actionable recovery plan. Settlements are never destroyed; no companion dies on default settings because of this event.

### Node 9 — The Road Stays Open

**Purpose:** make the consequences visible over time and let the player turn the one-off defense into lasting regional recovery.

**Return visit:** the player revisits the same road at dawn, dusk or after rain. Damage has not vanished between scenes. A guard is resting; a miller is short on grain; a goblin family is helping repair a lantern post. The player can hear thanks, frustration or disagreement tied to who was protected and what supplies were lost.

**Recovery choices:** repair the road marker, restore forage and den signs, replace lost food through trade, help the injured return to work or post seasonal warnings. These are small tasks with concrete results, not a rebuilding grind. The player may use crafting, gathering, cooking, negotiation or a purchased service; each contributes differently to safety, prosperity and local trade.

**Long-term result:** as the route recovers, joint contracts appear in the ordinary board pool and wildlife sightings shift to safer corridors. The player sees new produce at the market, a paired lantern at the road, and NPCs returning to routines. The joint patrol is optional content, not an endless emergency loop.

**Closing beat:** Sella asks whether the player will walk the first fully open route with them. If the player declines, they still have a warm goodbye and can meet again. If the player accepts, the camera follows them from behind for a few steps as the sun breaks through the canopy, then gives control back. This is a hopeful landing, not an ending that claims every problem is solved.

### Persistence rules

- Saving and reloading preserves evidence, promises, relationship state, summit preparation and regional damage/recovery.
- Leaving the region pauses the story event until the player returns; it never fires an unavoidable attack off-screen.
- If the player changes class, Sella's party role remains useful and the investigation retains non-class solutions.
- If Sella is absent from the party or the player chose a rival/enemy route, the patrol still functions through its established NPC participants. No story-critical role is locked behind a recruit decision.
- On default settings, companion death is opt-in. This arc contains no unavoidable companion death, and losing a defense does not delete a questline.
- If the player returns long after liberation, local NPC dialogue acknowledges elapsed time and recovery rather than replaying the original emergency as if it were new.

### Camera, animation and voice rules

1. Combat telegraphs stay visible; camera accents never hide an attack.
2. Dialogue close-ups emphasize eyes, hands and posture. Save the widest framing for the earned counterattack.
3. Sella's movement communicates state: they keep an exit in view when anxious and sit beside the player after trust grows.
4. Keep idle motion subtle and varied to avoid fatigue across long play sessions.
5. Hit-stop, speed lines and camera shake are brief and adjustable; subtitle and telegraph readability remain intact.
6. English is the first voice language. If full recording is out of scope, prioritize voiced combat calls and key emotional lines; all essential information remains in subtitles and animation.
7. No critical cue relies on audio alone. Use captions, distinct animation and text options for warning calls and uncertain translation.

Ambient sound establishes the before-and-after: Brindlewick's guarded bustle, wet leaves on the road, the Hush's silence, Hollowpine's workshop clatter, Millbrook's wheel, and a shared dawn chorus once the route is safe.

## Relationship outcomes

The relationship is accumulated, not decided by one dialogue choice:

Track a few understandable story dimensions rather than a hidden romance-like score: **Trust** (promises and honesty), **Respect** (whether the player listens and treats civilians fairly), **Evidence** (which claims are confirmed) and **Harm/Repair** (concrete consequences for both clans). Dialogue, companion availability and summit options can then reflect actions the player remembers taking.

- **Friend:** earned trust and a viable truce. Sella can become a long-term AI companion, with clan duties, independent opinions and party banter. They can challenge the player and may leave temporarily if the player repeatedly breaks promises to their people.
- **Rival:** the player and Sella respect each other’s skill but remain divided over methods. Sella appears as an independent ally in selected battles.
- **Enemy:** repeated harm to goblin civilians or deliberate falsification of evidence can make Sella oppose the player. Later choices can still create a path to repair the relationship; the initial split does not erase the arc.

Friendship is not automatic romance. Romance eligibility, if any, must follow the approved adult companion roster and the player’s chosen preferences.
